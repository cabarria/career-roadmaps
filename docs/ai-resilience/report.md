# AI resilience across Career Roadmaps

**Evidence cutoff:** November 25, 2026. **Geography:** United States unless stated otherwise. The scenario horizon is 2029–31, not an employment forecast. This is a research review for students and site maintainers; it does not change the production site.

**Comparison-page follow-up:** the site displays two published exposure indicators and BLS projected job growth independently, alongside qualitative task counts and expandable research profiles derived from the evidence and coverage ledgers. See [comparison-indicators.md](comparison-indicators.md). The decision below to withhold a composite resilience score is unchanged.

## Findings in brief

- All 28 featured occupations and 220 career cards are inventoried and assessed. The cards are education-to-career destinations, not uniformly defined occupations. A career card can span multiple employers, occupations, credential levels and seniority stages.
- Generative AI is already changing digital production, routine writing, programming, analysis and documentation; physical presence, judgment, interpersonal trust, legal accountability and verification often remain. High exposure can coincide with expanding demand or with a narrower entry route.
- Students should develop domain knowledge before delegating their practice, learn to verify generated work independently, and seek authentic supervised experience with people, systems or evidence. Employers need to preserve training opportunities as routine junior tasks shift.
- **Do not publish a 0–100 resilience ranking from this evidence.** The occupation profiles are useful; the published exposure, adoption, task importance and BLS outlook measurements cannot be converted into one defensible common resilience score.

## What resilience means

AI resilience is an occupation's capacity to retain demand for meaningful human work as AI changes its tasks. It is not a synonym for low technical exposure, low adoption or job security. Separate:

| Dimension | Meaning | Evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| Technical exposure | Tasks that a tool can assist, accelerate or complete | Current capability evaluations on representative work |
| Adoption | Whether workers and employers actually use it | Representative usage, integration, cost and constraints |
| Continuing human responsibility | What must still be done, checked, decided or owned by people | Tasks, work context, law, policy and real workflow observations |
| Demand | Whether additional output offsets labor savings | Actual hiring, wages, staffing and output demand response |

Traditional process automation, robotics, computer vision and predictive ML are considered separately from generative assistants and agents. The first may affect work independently of the latter. A benchmark, a survey, an actual usage log, a projection, and a causal labor effect are different evidence types.

## Method and reproducibility

1. Reconciled the catalog to the repository's `roadmapSummaries`, stable `careerId` values and 2025 BLS National Employment Matrix codes. Parsed the displayed career card titles and role ladders from each JSX artifact, and reviewed the matching research notes and existing AI discussion. The coverage ledger preserves card order and stable anchor IDs. Card IDs are locally assigned ordinal identifiers, not new production career IDs.
2. Mapped paths to best-fit **O*NET-SOC 2019** codes in release **31.0**, following [O*NET taxonomy](https://www.onetcenter.org/taxonomy.html) and [BLS 2018 SOC crosswalks](https://www.bls.gov/soc/2018/crosswalks.htm). A card may encompass more than its cited code; mapping quality is determined relative to the displayed pathway. **Exact** means the representative occupational family aligns; **narrower** means the path is a specialty or setting within the named occupation; **broader** means a card spans multiple listed occupations; **proxy** means the code is a useful partial analogue with material omissions; **unavailable** means a role is defined by ownership or otherwise not assignable to a unique occupational code. Multi-code lists are illustrative, not weighted estimates. An occupation is not a degree or a sector.
3. Retrieved version-pinned [Task Statements](https://www.onetcenter.org/dl_files/database/db_31_0_csv/task_statements.csv), [Task Ratings](https://www.onetcenter.org/dl_files/database/db_31_0_csv/task_ratings.csv), and [Occupation Data](https://www.onetcenter.org/dl_files/database/db_31_0_csv/occupation_data.csv). The task entries are joined by O*NET-SOC code and task ID. For one-code anchors, select six highest-rated core tasks by non-suppressed importance (IM, 1–5) except financial analysts, whose statements were available but associated usable IM ratings were not; choose six relevant tasks there. For broad anchors select two highest core tasks per available distinct component. Ties retain file order. The task sample is illustrative, not an exhaustive workday: importance is not duration, frequency categories are not time shares, and selected tasks do not sum to 100%. Additional codes are retained when missing occupational task data; the narrow examples do not imply full aggregation coverage.
4. The human-led, assisted and delegated-with-review labels are **researcher scenario judgments**, not results measured by O*NET or verified task-level performance for every occupation. The task column quotes source statements (O*NET license below); the verification and human responsibility column describes plausible boundaries using the occupational and sector evidence. The independent source rows specify how direct or indirect the supporting evidence is. The current evidence is strongest for bounded writing, consulting, software, tutoring, image generation and clinical documentation; other task judgments are analogies to those workflows and professional standards. Where evidence lacks direct occupation-specific observations, the relevant profile says so.
5. BLS [Table 1.2](https://www.bls.gov/emp/tables/occupational-projections-and-characteristics.htm) reports baseline 2025 employment and 2025–35 projections, cross-checked against the repository by code. Annual openings include replacement as well as growth. The projected numbers include economy-wide assumptions, and some forecasts explicitly consider AI; they are not estimates of AI's effect. The [Occupational Requirements Survey](https://www.bls.gov/ors/) provides useful context about job requirements but is not a task delegation or labor-displacement measure. The analysis uses no occupation-average hours or inferred time shares.

To reproduce the profile, find the exact occupation code and task IDs in `evidence.csv`, download the release files named above, select IM rows with `Recommend Suppress != Y`, and match task statements and date by code/ID. The coverage ledger contains every mapped card and the path assessment status. The source table below and the profile citations identify the relevant independent evidence and its limitations. Retain the release vintage when updating: O*NET rotates occupation updates, so individual dates differ. Confirm each match against the occupation definition rather than counting occupation-name string matches as validation.

### Special taxonomy cases

- **Psychologists (19-3030)** is a BLS summary, not a direct O*NET detailed code. Its 2025 component jobs (thousands) are 19-3032 I-O 4.0, 19-3033 clinical/counseling 81.3, 19-3034 school 64.0, 19-3039 all other 60.0. For a future component-level aggregate, normalized weights are each count divided by the sum 209.3 (rounding differs from 209.4 parent). The last component includes neuropsychology; examples from 19-3039.03 do not represent all other psychologists. No component scores are imputed.
- **Social workers (21-1020)** comprises 21-1021 child/family/school 405.1, 21-1022 healthcare 194.8, 21-1023 mental health/substance abuse 138.9, 21-1029 all other 77.4 (thousands). Normalized weights would divide each by 816.2 (rounding differs from parent 816.1). O*NET has no comparable detailed task statements for the residual 21-1029.00; it remains unmatched for task aggregation.
- **Data scientists (15-2051)** is one BLS line item but O*NET lists Data Scientists, Business Intelligence Analysts, and Clinical Data Managers under this code. No comparable matrix employment breakdown is available, so the profile shows separate task evidence with no weights. The same caution applies to O*NET subspecialties of civil and mechanical engineers, economists, writers, and RNs: parent task evidence is a general occupational profile, not a weighted subtype average. Additional subspecialty titles appear in the ledger. Do not double-count subordinate codes.
- Game art uses special effects artists/animators, including non-game visual media. Family medicine covers one physician specialty; biochemists/biophysicists and medical scientists cover only part of molecular biology and neuroscience, respectively, and include workers outside those disciplines. Philosophy faculty and political scientists are narrow job anchors. The report intentionally avoids degree-level risk claims.

## Current evidence and scenario across groups

| Group | Current pattern | 2029–31 conditional scenario |
|---|---|---|
| Arts, humanities and society | Commodity visual/text production is exposed; human expressive contribution, classroom management, therapeutic relationships and safeguarding persist. | Cheaper production may put pressure on freelance and junior craft work; human-led care and education could extend reach if institutions fund capacity. |
| Business, law and policy | Research, synthesis, valuation, presentations and document review can be assisted. Bounded experiments demonstrate gains, but errors, verification and professional duties matter. | Leaner junior research teams or expanded client coverage are both plausible; supervision, negotiation and implementation may absorb time. |
| Computing and technology | Coding assistance is common in self-selected surveys; productivity varies by task and repository context. The game-art proxy is not game programming. | Agents may execute more scoped tasks, but maintained systems, data quality, evaluation, human collaboration and product acceptance remain constraints. Entry-level work could reorganize substantially. |
| Architecture and engineering | Drafts, analysis and documentation are amenable to assistance; real-world measurements, safety and sign-off matter. Existing CAD and automation are separate. | Approved integrated agents accelerate designs and reviews, but infrastructure, demand, integration and validation determine staffing. |
| Health and life sciences | Documentation, summarization and research assistance are expanding. Device AI, robotics and predictive tools are different evidence. Clinical and research accountability remains. | Depending on validated workflows, reimbursement, privacy and budgets, clinicians and researchers may gain capacity or face higher output expectations rather than direct substitution. |
| Physical sciences and math | Automated data handling and computation are established; newer agents assist research, proof and code. | Cheaper computation could enable more work, while funding, instrument time, experimentation and verification continue to constrain jobs. |

Scenario assumptions: approved data access, integration into common work systems, materially improved but not infallible agents, review proportional to risk, and slowly changing professional obligations. No assumption of independently reliable autonomous practice or universal rollout. A faster-adoption variant removes routine drafting hours sooner; a slower one preserves more manual production, particularly in sensitive, physical, resource-constrained and regulated settings. Whether lower cost expands output enough to preserve labor demand is unresolved for every occupation.

### Employment and training disagreement

The Stanford ADP evidence documents divergence in employment by exposure and age (see [August 2026 paper, Figure C.11](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf)). Yet [Frank et al. (2026)](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/ai-exposed-jobs-deteriorated-before-chatgpt/) find some US exposure-linked employment deterioration predates ChatGPT. [Chandar and Klein Teeselink (2026)](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/how-does-ai-change-labor-demand/) give multinational evidence that the junior share in adopting affiliates declines mainly through expansion of senior employment. Neither workforce share nor occupational employment trends identify an individual career's probability of finding a job. International findings inform hypotheses, not quantitative US extrapolation. Employers may eliminate traditional apprenticeships faster than they eliminate entire professions; the effect on long-term skill formation warrants monitoring.

## Scoring decision

No candidate-dataset.json is emitted. Considered methods before constructing a ranking:

| Method | What is measured | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| ILO 2025 refined index (WP 140) | Technical exposure gradients derived from Polish task ratings, expert input and modeling on ISCO-08 | Valuable exposure evidence, not resilience; country and taxonomy mapping uncertainty. Reversing an exposure index does not measure demand. |
| OpenAI AI Jobs Transition Framework, Apr 2026 (PDF pp. 14–16, 21–25, 27–31) | Theoretical task exposure, observed ChatGPT use, human necessity and modeled price elasticity; four transition categories | Closest construct match; no validated 0–100 scalar. Underlying 2025 ChatGPT usage is proprietary, human necessity is rule-coded, and elasticity is GPT-estimated, not observed. Appendix states further methodological and robustness details will follow. No reproducible occupation-level inputs for the 28 anchors were verified. |
| Anthropic Economic Index, Jan 2026 | Observed Claude task conversations and model-classified primitives | Good evidence of how specific tools are used; not a probability of US occupation adoption or employment resilience. |
| 100 minus an exposure index or count of AI-assisted tasks | Inverse exposure, or fraction of chosen tasks under a review label | Reject as resilience. Selected task counts and importance ratings cannot establish hours or relative economic value. |
| Composite of exposure, BLS projection, licensure and adoption | Analyst-defined weighting | Reject: BLS projection mixes many forces, licensure is not inherently a demand guarantee, adoption denominators and time shares differ, and there are no defensible validated weights or missing-data thresholds. |

If the tab were modified to display **technical exposure**, use a published exposure measure with transparent mapping, vintage and limitations, labeled under its original construct and not substituted silently for resilience. OpenAI's four categories could be considered as unranked qualitative context if occupation rows and mapping can be reproduced. The existing tab expects finite numeric 0–100 values; neither method justifies filling that scale. We do not assign numeric resilience values, endpoints, or probabilities, so there is no score reproduction or ranking sensitivity test to perform.

To illustrate **why demand response matters**, assume a hypothetical occupation saves 10% of labor hours per unit while quality stays fixed. If output grows 5%, labor required relative to baseline = 0.90 × 1.05 = 0.945 (5.5% lower); if output grows 20%, relative labor is 0.90 × 1.20 = 1.08 (8% higher). These are arithmetic scenarios, not measured occupations or scores. At 10% savings a break-even output increase is 1/0.90 − 1 = 11.1%. Slower/faster adoption and substitution assumptions can reverse the direction. Consequently a single observed exposure fraction cannot determine resilience.

### Evidence required to reopen scoring

Obtain a common-date US dataset for representative occupation task-time/exposure and employer deployment rates (with sample and uncertainty); separate task delegation from oversight and validate capability on actual workflows. Quantify continuing human work by task duration and bottleneck, settings or licensing scope, then estimate labor hours per output and demand response using credible empirical measurements, not just projections. Require exact or documented weighted component matches and no silent missing/zero imputation. Publish indicators individually. Pre-register weights and endpoints against the resilience definition, validate against independently observed staffing and output changes, test ranges of adoption/demand and weights, and withhold any row without minimum evidence. Only then produce candidate data for review; do not blend different constructs to force coverage.
