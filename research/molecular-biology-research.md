# The Real State of AI in Molecular Biology: A Practitioner's Field Guide (June 2026)

## TL;DR
- AI is now a default part of the molecular biology toolkit — overall AI tool usage among researchers jumped from 57% in 2024 to 84% in 2025 (Wiley ExplanAItions survey of 2,430 researchers) — but its real role is as a fast, fallible assistant for hypothesis generation, code, structure prediction, and annotation, not an autonomous discoverer. The dominant practitioner stance is "useful assistant, never an oracle" (the recurring metaphors are "summer intern" and "tool, not replacement").
- The highest-utility, genuinely embedded tools today are AlphaFold2/3 and ColabFold (structure), ProteinMPNN/RFdiffusion (design), Benchling/CRISPOR/CHOPCHOP (CRISPR guides), ChatGPT/Claude/Copilot (code and writing), GPTCelltype and scVI/Scanpy/Seurat (single cell), and Elicit/Consensus/SciSpace (literature). Each solves a real bottleneck but carries documented failure modes — hallucinated packages and citations, intrinsically-disordered-region errors, unreliable binding-affinity ranking, and weak rare-cell annotation.
- The biggest gap between hype and reality is in drug discovery: AI accelerates early discovery and produced the first generative-AI drug to clear Phase 2a (Insilico's rentosertib, +98.4 mL FVC vs. −20.3 mL placebo in the GENESIS-IPF trial), but no AI-discovered drug has FDA approval as of late 2025, and the field's core problem — clinical success rates — remains unsolved.

## Key Findings

1. **Adoption is broad but shallow.** Most researchers use general-purpose tools (ChatGPT) rather than specialist ones. In the UK Engineering Biology survey, 69% of all respondents use AI tools but 52% only on an ad-hoc basis and just 7% report fully embedded use.\[1\] Trust in outputs is the single most-cited barrier (52% at organization level, 60% industry-wide).
2. **Structure prediction is the clearest win** — AlphaFold is used by over 3 million researchers\[2\] in more than 190 countries — but practitioners treat predictions as "exceptionally useful hypotheses," not experimental truth, and flag systematic failures on disordered regions, membrane proteins, novel folds, and ligand docking.
3. **Protein design is the fastest-moving frontier with genuine wet-lab validation** — RFdiffusion + ProteinMPNN is now a standard de novo design pipeline, recently extended to atomically accurate antibody design.
4. **CRISPR guide design is quietly mature** — deep-learning scoring (Doench/Fusi/Azimuth, DeepCRISPR) is baked into everyday platforms (Benchling, CRISPOR, CHOPCHOP) that researchers use routinely without thinking of them as "AI."
5. **Single-cell foundation models are hyped but underperform simple baselines** in independent zero-shot benchmarks; LLM-based annotation (GPTCelltype) is convenient but "not a one-size-fits-all solution."
6. **LLMs for code are the most universally adopted daily AI workflow** — and the most consistently complained about (hallucinated packages, "garbage that looks right").
7. **Literature tools save real time but fabricate** — retrieval-grounded tools (Elicit, Consensus, SciSpace) hallucinate less than raw chatbots but still misquote and over-rely on abstracts.

## Details

### 1. Protein structure prediction (AlphaFold and successors)

**Tools in actual use:** AlphaFold2 (local/ColabFold), AlphaFold3 via the AlphaFold Server, ESMFold (Meta), RoseTTAFold All-Atom (University of Washington), and open AF3 reimplementations Boltz-1/2 and Chai-1. ColabFold — which swaps AlphaFold2's slow homology search for MMseqs2 — is the practical workhorse for labs without large GPU clusters. AlphaFold is used by over 3 million researchers in more than 190 countries (including over 1 million users in low- and middle-income countries), and the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database holds over 200 million predicted structures. In the UK survey it was the second most-adopted tool after ChatGPT (20% of non-vendor users).

**Daily workflow:** Researchers generate predicted structures from sequence to (a) form mechanistic hypotheses before the bench, (b) fit and interpret ambiguous cryo-EM density, (c) guide mutagenesis — for example, simulating alanine scanning to find interface hot spots, then validating only the reduced subset — and (d) screen which phosphorylation site might cause a conformational switch. The workflow inverts the traditional one: predict computationally, then validate selectively.

**What it solves vs. limits:** It collapses what was months of experimental structure determination into a weekend for well-behaved globular proteins. But practitioners are emphatic about the limits. A Berkeley Lab/*Nature Methods* study led by Tom Terwilliger (New Mexico Consortium) concluded predictions "are best considered to be exceptionally useful hypotheses, and that experimental measurements remain essential for confirmation,"\[3\] finding even highest-confidence models carry roughly twice the error of experimental structures. Co-author Paul Adams: "If a scientist is studying the evolution of proteins by making global comparisons, the AlphaFold models are very useful… However, if a researcher wants to study ligand docking for structure-based drug design, there is no substitute for experimental data."\[3\] Protein biophysicist Julie Forman-Kay (University of Toronto) warned: "There's this sense now that people don't have to do experimental structure determination, which is not true."\[4\] Documented systematic failures: intrinsically disordered regions (AF3 produces non-deterministic "hallucinations" on IDPs), membrane proteins (inconsistent transmembrane placement), novel folds lacking homologs, and protein–ligand/cofactor interactions.

**AlphaFold3 backlash:** AF3's May 2024 launch as a web-server-only tool — no code initially, capped at roughly 10–20 jobs/day, non-commercial license, finite ligand list with no custom small molecules — triggered an outcry. Over 1,000 scientists signed a protest letter.\[5\] UCSF's Stephanie Wankowicz described "emails flying"\[5\] between structural biologists within an hour ("how can they make claims like this?"). DeepMind subsequently released AF3 code and weights for academic use in November 2024. Enthusiast counterpoint: King's College London's Julien Bergeron said his lab "uses it daily" and praised the paper for flagging its own shortcomings. A common ground-level frustration is mundane: ColabFold crashes on large proteins when free Colab GPUs run out of RAM — a Biostars user could not fold a ~1,500-residue protein even on a 1 TB workstation, and the free T4 GPU effectively caps out around 2,400 residues.

### 2. Genomics and gene expression analysis

**Tools in actual use:** DNA language/foundation models — Enformer and Borzoi (expression and chromatin accessibility from sequence), Evo and Evo 2 (Arc Institute/Stanford; 7B and 40B parameters, up to 1-million-token context, generative design across DNA/RNA/protein), Nucleotide Transformer, DNABERT-2, and HyenaDNA. For variant interpretation, AlphaMissense (DeepMind) is the headline AI tool.

**Daily workflow:** Foundation-model embeddings feed downstream prediction tasks — enhancer/promoter/splice-site identification, functional annotation, and variant effect. AlphaMissense scores are pulled into clinical and research pipelines to triage the "needle-in-a-haystack" problem of Variants of Unknown Significance, often combined with phenotype-driven tools such as Phenolyzer.

**What it solves vs. limits:** AlphaMissense correlates better with functional (multiplexed assay of variant effect, or MAVE) data than earlier predictors and reaches high specificity — 94.1% across inherited retinal disease genes, and 100% in ABCA4, USH2A, RPGR, and PRPH2 — with a 9.6% false discovery rate. But it has real limits: it underperforms on hypomorphic variants (notably ABCA4-associated Stargardt disease), lacks interpretability, does not assess functional mechanism, and is not disease-specific — so it informs but cannot replace ACMG clinical classification. Evo's genome-scale generation is described by its own creators (Brian Hie) as "the most futuristic and crazy" part of the work — generating mock bacterial genomes that carry many but not all genes a cell needs; this is research-frontier, not daily workflow.

### 3. CRISPR guide RNA design and gene editing

**Tools in actual use:** CRISPOR, CHOPCHOP, Benchling's CRISPR module, CRISPick (Broad Institute), Cas-OFFinder, Cas-Designer, and Synthego/IDT design tools. Most embed deep-learning/ML on-target efficacy scoring (Doench/Fusi/Azimuth 2016)\[6\] and off-target prediction. DeepCRISPR was an early unified deep-learning platform that combined on- and off-target prediction, reporting a roughly twofold improvement in Spearman correlation over the prior state-of-the-art on-target tool.

**Daily workflow:** This is the most "invisible AI" area — researchers design guides routinely without calling it AI. A practitioner's honest recommendation (BioTech Bench, 2026): "for most academic labs, start with CRISPOR. It's free, it's fast, and it covers almost every genome… If your lab uses Benchling (it's free for academics) — use the CRISPR module. The integration alone makes it worth it."\[7\] Standard practice is to cross-check guides through at least two tools: "I usually run my guides through at least two tools before committing."\[7\] Benchling lets you design guides inside the same environment as sequences, plasmid maps, and the electronic notebook (off-target search runs Cas-OFFinder under the hood), then assemble into plasmids — eliminating CSV copy-pasting and batching guide design up to 100× faster than competitors. CRISPOR supports over 400 genomes and outputs cloning primers for common vectors; CRISPRscan is the go-to for zebrafish, fly, and worm because its scoring model was trained on injection data from those systems rather than human/mouse cell lines.

**What it solves vs. limits:** Predicts which guides will be efficient and specific before wet-lab testing, saving rounds of screening. Limits: scoring models trained on human/mouse cell lines generalize imperfectly to other organisms, and practitioners are explicitly warned not to rely solely on any single tool's proprietary scoring — "don't rely solely on proprietary scoring from either — cross-check with CRISPOR if the experiment matters."

### 4. Drug discovery and molecular docking

**Tools in actual use:** DiffDock (MIT; diffusion-based pose prediction), NVIDIA BioNeMo, Boltz-1/Boltz-2 (MIT/Recursion; structure plus binding affinity, MIT-licensed open source), Chai-1, AlphaFold3, plus physics-based incumbents (Schrödinger Glide, FEP, UCSF DOCK). Boltz-2 was trained on roughly 5 million binding-affinity measurements and topped the CASP16 affinity challenge.

**Daily workflow:** AI co-folding/docking is used for virtual screening (screening thousands of compounds in hours rather than weeks), hit identification, pose prediction, and lead prioritization — typically as a first-pass filter feeding physics-based refinement (molecular dynamics, FEP) and then experiment.

**What it solves vs. limits:** Speed and accessibility for pose generation. But independent evaluation is sobering. An arXiv benchmark on the SARS-CoV-2 main protease (3CLPro) and tankyrase-2 (TNKS2), using datasets of 16,780 and 21,702 compounds, concluded that while Boltz-2 is useful for generating complexes "when canonical docking approaches are unfeasible, its poor performance in affinity ranking indicates that it cannot serve as a reliable substitute for high-precision, physics-based methods in late-stage drug discovery."\[8\] A Nature/*npj Drug Discovery* study found AI co-folding can separate active from inactive compounds, but with concordance varying wildly by system (concordance indices from 0.52, no meaningful correlation, to 0.89). DiffDock degrades on large ligands or multi-chain complexes outside its training distribution.

**The hype-vs-reality headline:** AI accelerates early discovery but has not solved drug development's core problem. On June 3, 2025, Insilico Medicine published in *Nature Medicine* the first peer-reviewed Phase 2a result for a drug with both target and molecule generated by AI: rentosertib (ISM001-055), a TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, developed on Insilico's Pharma.AI platform. In the 71-patient, 22-site GENESIS-IPF trial (conducted in China), the 60 mg once-daily cohort showed a mean forced vital capacity (FVC) increase of +98.4 mL versus a mean decline of −20.3 mL for placebo over 12 weeks. The lead investigator, Dr. Zuojun Xu of Peking Union Medical College, cautioned: "the sample size in each patient group was relatively limited, and these findings will need to be validated in larger cohort studies." As of December 2025, no AI-discovered drug has FDA approval. Recursion discontinued its lead AI candidate REC-994 in May 2025 after long-term data did not confirm earlier efficacy trends. Medicinal chemist Derek Lowe's framing: drug development's ~85% clinical failure rate\[9\] stems from poor target selection and human toxicity prediction — "the two biggest hurdles… not yet within AI's reach."\[9\] Industry analyst Raminderpal Singh called 2025 "not a revolution but a sobering reality check."

### 5. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

**Tools in actual use:** Scanpy (Python/scverse, the scalable workhorse for million-cell datasets), Seurat (R), scVI (deep generative model for integration and batch correction), Harmony (integration), SingleR and SCINA (reference- and marker-based annotation), and Squidpy (spatial). Single-cell foundation models: scGPT, Geneformer, scBERT, CellPLM, and UCE. LLM-based annotation: GPTCelltype (GPT-4 wrapped in an R package that integrates with Seurat).

**Daily workflow:** The standard pipeline is QC → normalization → clustering → annotation. The labor-intensive step is cell-type annotation; GPTCelltype automates it by feeding the marker genes for each cluster to GPT-4. scVI and Harmony handle batch integration; Scanpy and Seurat handle the rest.

**What it solves vs. limits:** GPTCelltype's developers (Hou & Ji, *Nature Methods* 2024) report strong concordance with manual annotation (over 75% agreement), reference-free operation, low cost, and no coding required. But independent practitioners are skeptical. A replication study ("Beyond the Hype," bioRxiv 2025) concluded "GPT-4 is not a one-size-fits-all solution for cell type annotation," gives "average performance in all contexts," defaults to generic labels (stromal/fibroblast) for heterogeneous cells, scored as low as 0.08 on a dataset with rarer cell types, and that "traditional methods specifically trained on specific tissues and cell-types will outperform GPT-4."\[10\] More damning for the foundation-model hype: a *Genome Biology* zero-shot benchmark found scGPT and Geneformer "perform worse than selecting highly variable genes (HVG) and using more established methods such as Harmony and scVI in cell type clustering,"\[11\] and that larger pretraining datasets did not reliably improve performance. The community's response has been to build guardrails — CellTypeAgent cross-checks GPT against the CellxGene database, and consensus-voting wrappers have emerged — rather than trust raw LLM output, because LLMs "suffer from hallucinations and can generate inconsistent outputs for the same query" and "lack sufficient uncertainty quantification." Practitioner verdict: a useful first pass that must be validated by a domain expert.

### 6. Literature review and hypothesis generation using LLMs

**Tools in actual use:** ChatGPT/Claude (general), Elicit (paper screening and extraction, systematic review), Consensus (evidence summaries with a "consensus meter"),\[12\] SciSpace (chat-with-PDF, 280M+ paper database, popular with graduate students), Perplexity (quick cited context), Scite (citation classification), Litmaps/Connected Papers (citation networks), and NotebookLM (source-grounded synthesis).

**Daily workflow:** A widely-shared practitioner list ("7 AI tools I actually use… No hype. No affiliates"): Elicit for first-pass screening, Consensus for "what does the research say about X," SciSpace to decode dense methods sections, Litmaps for citation networks, Perplexity for 60-second background, and Claude for synthesis drafts. Researchers also use ChatGPT/Claude for hypothesis brainstorming and as an interactive explainer for papers outside their expertise.

**What it solves vs. limits:** Cuts initial screening time dramatically and makes unfamiliar literature accessible. But fabrication is the defining risk. General-purpose LLMs fabricate citations at alarming rates — one ChatGPT-3.5 audit of 115 medical references (Bhattacharyya et al., Cureus 2023) found 47% fabricated, 46% authentic but inaccurate, and only 7% authentic and accurate (with an incorrect PMID in 93% of references); another study (Bhattacharya et al., medRxiv 2023) found 41 of 59 references (69%) fabricated. Retrieval-grounded tools hallucinate less because they cite indexed papers, but an HKUST Library evaluation of Elicit, Consensus, Scite, and Scopus AI found they still "inaccurately conclude based on… abstracts instead of specific findings," quote secondary sources, and in one case "almost copied word-for-word from a source's abstract… which could result in plagiarism." Wiley's survey found researcher concern about inaccuracies and hallucinations rose from 51% to 64% year-over-year as firsthand experience grew.

### Code generation (cross-cutting daily workflow)

This deserves its own note because it is arguably the most universal daily AI use in computational biology. Tools: ChatGPT, Claude, GitHub Copilot, OpenAI Codex, and gptstudio (for R/RStudio). Scientists spend roughly 30–35% of their time developing software, and LLMs now write, debug, refactor, and translate scripts. In a controlled test (Piccolo et al., *PLOS Computational Biology* 2023), ChatGPT solved 139 of 184 introductory-bioinformatics programming exercises (75.5%) on the first attempt and 179 (97.3%) within seven attempts. The honest practitioner consensus is that it is a useful "summer intern"\[13\] (bioinformatician Xijin Ge's phrase — hard-working and eager but inexperienced and error-prone) that needs supervision. Biostars users are blunt: "ChatGPT seems to be very good at posting answers which sound and look right but are actually a load of garbage," and "it wildly makes up papers that do not even exist but with great confidence."\[14\] The recurring warning from a computational-biology analysis: "If you are not familiar with a library that ChatGPT is using, definitely verify… there is a slight chance of it being made-up… Even wrong code generated by ChatGPT comes with comments which make the code look well documented, leading to misplaced confidence in the code." GitHub Copilot's average suggestion acceptance rate sits around 30%.

### Adoption data summary
- **Wiley ExplanAItions** (2,430 researchers, August 2025): overall AI usage rose 57% → 84% (2024→2025); research/publication-specific use 45% → 62%; 85% say it improved efficiency; but 80% use mainstream tools (ChatGPT) versus 25% specialist tools; only 11% had heard of the specialized research tools surveyed; hallucination concern rose 51% → 64%, privacy concern 47% → 58%.
- **UK Engineering Biology survey** (368 respondents, June 2025; commissioned by DSIT): 69% use AI; 52% ad-hoc, only 7% fully embedded; top tools ChatGPT (30%) and AlphaFold (20%); university users also use RFdiffusion (8%), Chai-1 (5%), and ESM (5%); top applications are conversational assistance (89%), background research (83%), and drafting (73%), with hypothesis testing far lower at 42%; trust in outputs is the top barrier (52% organization-level, 60% industry-wide); over half (54%) cannot identify what skills or training are needed.
- A nationally representative US survey (*PLOS One* 2025, 232 STEM respondents) found 65% had used generative AI in teaching or research, with 20% applying it in both. A 2025 single-institute survey found 43.2% used AI to "help write code," with a notable gender gap (women more likely to report being unfamiliar with available tools).

## Recommendations

**For an individual researcher deciding where to invest now:**

1. **Adopt immediately (high utility, low risk, mature):** ColabFold/AlphaFold for structure hypotheses; CRISPOR plus Benchling/CHOPCHOP for guide design (cross-check at least two tools); ChatGPT/Claude/Copilot for code with mandatory human review; Elicit/Consensus for literature triage with citation verification. These have clear ROI and well-understood failure modes.

2. **Adopt with guardrails:** GPTCelltype for a first-pass annotation, but always validate against marker-based tools (SingleR/SCINA) and a domain expert, especially for rare or novel cell types. AlphaMissense for variant triage, but never as a standalone clinical classifier — keep it within ACMG workflows.

3. **Watch but don't depend on (frontier, not yet daily-reliable):** single-cell foundation models (scGPT, Geneformer) — benchmark them against scVI/Harmony on your own data before trusting them; AI binding-affinity prediction (Boltz-2) — use it for pose generation and triage, not late-stage ranking; Evo/Evo 2 generative genomics — research-frontier.

4. **Calibrate expectations on drug discovery:** treat AI as an early-discovery accelerator, not a clinical-success solution. The benchmark that would change this: a Phase 3 success and FDA approval of an AI-discovered drug (watch rentosertib's larger trials and the AI-designed candidates entering pivotal trials in 2026).

**Thresholds that should change your behavior:**
- If a structure prediction falls in a disordered or membrane region, or has low pLDDT → do not trust it; get experimental data.
- If an LLM cites a paper or names a package you cannot independently verify → assume it is fabricated until confirmed.
- If a single-cell foundation model doesn't beat HVG+Harmony/scVI on your own dataset → use the simpler, established method.

## Caveats

- **The forum/practitioner voice here is partly proxied through Biostars** (the field's main Q&A forum) and on-record quotes in trade press (GEN, C&EN, *Science* news), because direct Reddit thread retrieval was not reliably available during research. The Biostars quotes are verbatim and attributable; treat them as representative of, but not a complete census of, ground-level opinion.
- **Survey representativeness is limited.** The UK Engineering Biology survey had a 1.42% response rate (368 responses) and explicitly is not statistically representative. The Wiley survey is publisher-commissioned. Treat all percentages as directional, not precise.
- **Tool-comparison blogs from 2025–2026 are SEO/marketing-adjacent** and were down-weighted; their "low hallucination" claims for literature tools are not backed by published benchmarks.
- **This field moves monthly.** Specific model versions, benchmarks, and clinical readouts cited here are accurate to approximately June 2026 and will shift.
- **Vendor claims and independent evaluation diverge consistently.** In nearly every area — GPTCelltype, scGPT, Boltz-2, AlphaFold3 — developer-reported performance was deflated by independent replication. Weight independent benchmarks over press releases.
