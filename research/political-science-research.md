## TL;DR

- The political science major is flexible and has no professional credential attached. Departments organize it around four constant subfields (American, Comparative, International Relations, Political Theory) plus a fifth that varies by school (Methodology, Political Economy, or Public Policy). A methods or statistics course is required at Yale, Berkeley, and Duke.
- Law is the largest single draw on the major. LSAC's 2015-16 data show political science supplied 12,693 law-school applicants (18.1%), more than any other major. That is a separate professional degree, and the data are a decade old.
- The one BLS occupation named "political scientist" is tiny and shrinking. BLS (May 2025) reports 6,100 jobs, a $142,080 median, a 2% decline projected through 2035, about 400 openings a year, and a master's degree as typical entry education.
- Master's funding is uneven. A few programs (Princeton SPIA, Notre Dame, NC State, Fordham) fund students fully through assistantships, and ProFellow warns that not all programs do. The reliably funded seat is the PhD.
- The academic job market is hard. In APSA's 2024-25 survey of 181 job-market candidates from 31 institutions, a little over a third took tenure-track jobs, nearly half took non-tenure-track jobs, and about 13% went outside academia.
- Several figures that circulate in career guides could not be verified and were removed from the roadmap, including a "51% underemployed in year one" claim that traces to one vendor page.

## Key Findings

- **Subfields are not uniform.** UNC and Michigan count Methodology as a subfield (S5, S6), Penn adds Political Economy (S1), and BU counts Public Policy (S7). American, Comparative, International Relations, and Political Theory appear everywhere.
- **Majors are 9 to 12 courses.** Penn and Yale require 12 (S1, S2), Berkeley 12 including one history course (S4), BU 11 (S7), BC 10 (S8), UNC 9 (S5).
- **Methods requirements are common.** Yale requires one methodology and formal theory course (S2). Berkeley requires PS 3 or Data Science C8 plus PS 88 (S4). Duke requires a statistics course (S9). UNC offers a Data in Politics track (S5).
- **Political science is the largest source of law-school applicants**, at 12,693 (18.12%) in LSAC's 2015-16 cycle (S48).
- **BLS "political scientists" is small:** 6,100 jobs, median $142,080 (May 2025), 10th percentile under $83,350, 90th over $195,190, minus 2% projected 2025-35, about 400 openings a year (S37).
- **Tenure-track placement is about one in three** among APSA's 2024-25 job-market candidates (S28).
- **Faculty prestige concentration is old data.** The oft-quoted 34% of political science faculty from five top programs traces to Burris (2004), cited second-hand in a 2025 arXiv paper (S27).
- **Faculty pay (APSA 2024-25 averages):** assistant $78,990, tenured associate $91,630, full professor $114,750. The brief labels the table "per pay term" and a chart "USD/year" without explaining the difference (S29).
- **Text-as-data and LLM annotation are active, and validation is stressed.** Published work calls for careful model selection and robust validation, and argues codebook definition is still a first-order concern (S30, S32).
- **AI exposure is sourced for only two career paths** (law and political data). The other six cards carry reasoned estimates, labeled as such in the JSX.

## Undergraduate

### Core structure

- **Subfield distribution:** Penn requires three of four subfields (S1). BC requires one course in each of four subfields beyond the introductory courses (S8). Michigan requires one course in each of four of five subfields (S6).
- **Yale (12 term courses):** two introductory courses, one methodology and formal theory course, two core lectures, two courses in each of two subfields, and two seminars including one in senior year. A senior seminar or senior essay is required (S2).
- **Berkeley (12 courses):** two introductory courses, PS 3 (or Data Science C8 plus PS 88), one approved history course, a subfield specialization (introductory course plus two upper-division), and eight upper-division courses. All must be taken for a letter grade (S4).
- **UNC:** nine courses, five subfields including methodology. Introductory course before advanced work in a subfield is advised (S5).
- **BU:** 11 courses (three core, seven electives, one seminar numbered 505-598 that must be taken on the Charles River campus) (S7).
- **Duke:** the statistics requirement (STA 198L or 199L, or ECON 104D) plus a political theory course such as POLSCI 175, plus a chosen concentration (S9).
- **Penn:** recommends one or two 300- or 400-level seminars (S1).

### The weeder question

None of the retrieved sources name a political science course as a weeder. What they show are gating mechanisms: Berkeley's upper-division courses fill before Phase 2 registration and only declared majors can enroll in Phase 1, so declaring by March of sophomore year matters (S4). Any claim that a particular course "weeds out" majors is anecdote not sourced here.

### Skills and methods

- **Quantitative methods** are taught as named courses. Columbia lists Applied Regression and Causal Inference, Experimental Methods, Politics in the Lab, and Design and Analysis of Sample Surveys (S3).
- **Formal theory** appears at Yale (grouped with methodology) (S2), Columbia (Logic of Collective Choice) (S3), and UNC (mathematical models of political decision making) (S5), and Mizzou trains PhD students in formal modeling and game theory (S24).
- **Programming language:** one career-guide source says political data analyst roles need "some familiarity with R or Python" (S38). No retrieved source ranks R against Stata or names specific R packages.
- **Writing and argumentation:** the same career guide ties the major's reading, writing, and argument skills to the LSAT (S38). No curriculum page states this.

### Beyond the classroom

- **Internships:** UNC offers credit for government and public-service internships (POLI 193, pass/fail, not counted among the nine major courses) (S5). Extern calls internships the biggest single factor in landing a degree-relevant first job (S38).
- **Research:** UNC's mentored research course (POLI 395) (S5), Yale's senior essay (S2), and Duke's graduation with distinction by original paper (S9). Duke suggests starting to think about the thesis in the second semester of junior year (S9).
- **Honor society:** Pi Sigma Alpha at UNC requires 15 hours of political science with a 3.3 overall and 3.5 political science GPA (S5).
- **Study abroad:** UNC encourages it for all students and strongly urges it for those interested in comparative politics and international relations (S5).
- **Not sourced:** Model UN, moot court, and campaign work as activities were not covered by any retrieved source beyond Extern's list of BA-accessible campaign roles (S38).

## Master's

### The degree fork

- **MPP** emphasizes policy analysis, economics, statistics, and program evaluation. **MPA** emphasizes management, budgeting, personnel, and public law (S13). Teach.com and Wikipedia both note the two curricula increasingly overlap (S10, S13).
- **MIA or MIR (international affairs)** covers history, economics, and security factors in foreign policy (S10). Emerging Tech Policy Careers calls MPA and MIA degrees "fairly broad and general, while offering some specializations" (S11).
- **Academic research master's** (Master of Political Science, Master of Social Sciences): Pepperdine's program page, which is a competitor's marketing page, describes these as rarely terminal bridges to doctoral study (S14).
- **JD** is a separate professional path (see Career Paths, Law).
- **Credential signal:** Emerging Tech Policy Careers says school name matters more than the letters (MA, MPP, MPA, MIA), which employers treat as "alphabet soup" (S11).

### Funding and admissions

- **Not all master's programs fund students** (S15). ProFellow defines "full funding" as full or partial tuition waiver plus a living stipend, normally tied to a 10 to 20 hour per week assistantship (S15).
- **Fully funded examples** from ProFellow: Princeton SPIA (MPP and MPA), Notre Dame (Global Affairs), NC State (International Studies, via assistantships), and Fordham (International Political Economy and Development) (S15). Emerging Tech Policy Careers separately cites generous funding at Princeton and Yale (S11).
- **Work experience tiers:** Princeton's one-year MPP requires at least seven years of public or nonprofit experience and enrolls about 20 students a class (S12). Rutgers' MPAP requires five or more years in politics or public policy (S18). Harvard's one-year MC/MPA is for established professionals (S17).
- **No tuition figures** were retrieved. The roadmap previously claimed "sticker prices clear six figures" without a source.

### Skills

- The MPP core includes microeconomics, public finance, statistics and advanced data analysis, qualitative research, and program evaluation (S13).
- Harvard Kennedy School offers a STEM-designated Data and Research Methods track across its MPP, MPA, and MC/MPA programs (S17).
- Princeton notes that mastery of a foreign language is often needed for careers and funds language study alongside the summer internship (S12).
- Miami's MPP requires an internship or applied capstone (S16). Princeton's MPP requires a pre-term summer program, a values-based leadership course, and presenting at policy forums (S12).

## PhD / Research

### Structure

- **Length:** 5 to 7 years in one overview (S23). Berkeley describes three years to candidacy plus two or three for the dissertation (S20). Fieldwork students at GW typically take six years (S19).
- **Coursework:** Berkeley requires 40 units and competence in three of nine subfields (S20). GW requires at least five courses in the primary field and four in the supporting field (S25).
- **Comprehensive exams:** two fields at GW (S25), one major and one minor at Johns Hopkins (two-day major, one-day minor) (S21). Mizzou students typically sit exams in spring of year three (S24). Stanford requires a first-field exam and a field paper, with candidacy by spring of year two (S26).
- **Failure consequences vary:** at GW, a student who does not earn a satisfactory pass on the second attempt at the major exam, or a bare pass on the minor, is terminated (S19). This is one school's rule.
- **Prospectus:** defended in an open forum at GW (S25). Berkeley expects candidacy by the end of year three (S20). Stanford requires a prospectus presentation in spring of year three (S26).
- **Dissertation:** Albany lets students choose a book-format dissertation or three article-format papers with an introduction and conclusion, and says both take similar effort (S22).
- **Teaching:** Stanford requires at least three quarters of teaching by the end of year four (S26).

### Funding

- GW guarantees five years, conditional on progress, and expects students to go on the market in fall of year five (S19).
- Albany: most students receive three to four years of funding with a full tuition waiver and stipend (S22).
- One overview says most top-tier programs offer full packages (tuition remission, stipend, health insurance) in exchange for teaching or research assistantships (S23).

### Placement

- **APSA 2024-25 (S28):** among 181 job-market candidates from 31 institutions, surveyed November to December 2025, a little over a third were placed in tenure-track jobs, nearly half in non-tenure-track roles, and about 13% in non-academic jobs. The page gives approximate shares only and does not report postdocs. These are candidates on the market, not all graduates.
- **Prestige concentration (S27):** a 2025 arXiv paper cites Burris (2004) for 34% of political science faculty coming from the top five programs, and Wapman et al. (2022) for five US universities having trained one in eight tenure-track faculty across fields. Both are older, second-hand figures.
- **Not found:** a recent political-science-specific figure for the share of PhDs who eventually hold tenure-track jobs. An anthropology study (about 79% without tenure-track jobs) turned up in searches but does not apply to political science (S57).

### Research frontiers (as supported by retrieved sources)

- **Quantitative design:** Columbia's sequence includes causal inference and experimental methods (S3). Mizzou lists experiments, spatial econometrics, and big-data collection including text analysis (S24).
- **Text-as-data and LLMs:** Grimmer and Stewart's framing and rapid adoption of automated text methods (S30), few-shot GPT classification of political documents (Ornstein et al.) and LLM placement of texts on ideological scales (Le Mens and Gallego) (S34), open-source models enabling reproducible work on populism (S33).
- **Substantive research cores named by departments:** Mizzou lists race and ethnic politics, voting behavior, political movements, civil wars, American political institutions, and conflict management (S24). GW dissertation topics include the partisan gender gap, judicial politics in the Middle East, media freedom in Turkey, and coercive kidnappings by violent political organizations (S25).
- **Democratic backsliding:** review articles exist in PS: Political Science and Politics (2023) and the Annual Review of Political Science, plus a Stanford CDDRL paper on a unified approach (S35, S36). These were seen as titles in search results only, so the roadmap states only that the area is active.
- **Formal theory:** Mizzou formal modeling and game theory (S24), Columbia Logic of Collective Choice (S3).

## Tools and AI

- **Standard tools:** R or Python for data analysis (S38). Beyond that, the retrieved curricula name courses, not software.
- **LLMs for coding political text:** Törnberg's guide presents LLMs as cheap, fast, and applicable to annotation, classification, and sentiment analysis (S31). Ornstein et al. and Le Mens and Gallego report strong results on classification and scaling tasks (S34).
- **Practitioner caution:** one comparison of 12 models says robust validation and domain knowledge are needed (S30). A 2025 paper argues codebook conceptualization remains a first-order concern in the LLM era (S32).
- **AI in careers:** only law (S46: routine legal work automated, new demand for AI-regulation expertise) and political data (S30-S34) have source support. The other cards carry estimates.

## Career Paths

All salary figures are US dollars. BLS figures are May 2025 data (S37, S49-S52) unless noted. See the audit section for figures that changed after gap-fill checks.

### 1. Academia

- **Titles:** Adjunct, Assistant Professor, Associate Professor (tenured), Full Professor.
- **Reality:** In APSA's 2024-25 survey, a little over a third of job-market candidates took tenure-track jobs and nearly half took non-tenure-track roles (S28). Prestige concentration data are two decades old (S27).
- **Salary:** APSA 2024-25 averages: assistant $78,990, tenured associate $91,630, full professor $114,750, postdoctoral fellow $62,110, full-time non-tenure-track $65,010 (S29). Pay basis is unclear in the brief.
- **Conflicting figure:** none retrieved. The earlier "$120K-$165K+ full professor (AAUP)" came from no retrieved source.

### 2. Government and policy analysis

- **Titles:** Research Assistant, Policy Analyst, Senior Analyst, Policy Director. Senior policy analyst median about $84,700 and director of public policy about $96,850 per Payscale as cited by Claremont Lincoln (S39).
- **Reality:** BLS "political scientists" are about 6,100 jobs, a 2% projected decline, about 400 openings a year, master's typical (S37). A secondary source says D.C. holds more than half of positions and political scientists are about 115 times more concentrated there than nationally (S40). Employers include agencies, lobbying groups, businesses, nonprofits, consulting firms, and special-interest groups (S39).
- **Salary:** BLS median $142,080, 10th percentile under $83,350, 90th over $195,190 (S37). PayScale reports an average of $67,576 for policy analyst with a range of $50K to $97K (S41). Glassdoor reports $108,432 average (S42). Claremont Lincoln cites a median of about $64,950 (S39).
- **Conflict:** the BLS category and the aggregator figures describe different populations. The roadmap shows both.

### 3. Law and the courts

- **Titles:** JD Student, Associate Attorney, Partner, Judge or General Counsel.
- **Reality:** Political science supplied the most law-school applicants in LSAC's 2015-16 data (S48). NALP: 93.4% of 2024 law graduates were employed within 10 months (S47). A federal hiring freeze has narrowed the government-lawyer channel (S46). Public-interest lawyers start near $60K (S38).
- **Salary:** BLS lawyers median $159,670, 10th percentile $78,360, 90th over $351,600 (S44). NALP 2025 median first-year $200,000, $150,000 at firms of 250 or fewer lawyers, $215,000 at firms over 700 (S45, S46). $225,000 is the most commonly reported first-year figure but not the median (S46).

### 4. Diplomacy and international affairs

- **Titles:** Foreign Service Officer candidate, Foreign Service Officer, Program Officer, Country Director.
- **Reality:** Becoming an officer requires passing the Foreign Service Officer Test (S38). State's entry rules place a bachelor's holder with up to five years of qualifying experience at FP-6, steps 5 to 10 (S54). ProFellow ties international affairs master's degrees to Foreign Service, diplomacy, and international NGO careers (S15).
- **Salary:** Extern gives $55K to $100K+ for officers (S38). State's own salary table was not retrieved (S54 has grade and step rules only).
- **Not sourced:** selectivity of the FSOT and oral assessment, intake size, hardship postings, UN hiring.

### 5. Campaigns, consulting, and lobbying

- **Titles:** Field Organizer, Campaign Manager, Political Consultant, Government-Relations Director.
- **Reality:** Campaign staff and legislative aides are BA-accessible (S38). Corporate government-relations directors reach $90K to $130K (S38).
- **Salary:** field organizer median $49,523, 10th to 90th percentile $35,891 to $59,545, updated 2026-11-01 (S53). Glassdoor reports an average of $160,945 for political consultants and $105K for government relations (S43, S42), both self-reported.
- **Not sourced:** campaign hours, lobbyist pay.

### 6. Political data and polling

- **Titles:** Data Analyst, Survey Methodologist, Pollster or Data Scientist, Analytics Director.
- **Reality:** Extern reports record data-infrastructure spending in the 2024 cycle and $55K to $80K for political data analysts who know R or Python (S38). The closest BLS occupation, survey researchers, has about 9,800 jobs, is projected to decline 5% through 2035, and typically requires a master's (S51).
- **Salary:** $55K to $80K analyst (S38). BLS survey researcher median $69,460, 10th percentile under $39,260, 90th over $130,860 (S51).

### 7. Journalism and communications

- **Titles:** Reporter, Political Correspondent, Communications Director, Press Secretary.
- **Reality:** BLS reporters and news analysts: bachelor's typical, median $62,200 (under $36,240 at the 10th percentile, over $144,140 at the 90th), projected to shrink 6% through 2035 (S49). Public relations specialists: median $74,750, projected to grow 2% (S50).
- **Salary:** $62,200 (reporters) and $74,750 (PR specialists). Communications-director and press-secretary pay was not sourced.

### 8. Nonprofit and advocacy

- **Titles:** Program Coordinator, Policy Associate, Advocacy Director, Executive Director.
- **Reality:** Nonprofit program coordinator roles are BA-accessible (S38). Extern puts BA holders at about $38K at the start and $52K to $93K at mid-to-late career (S38). BLS social and community service managers: median $80,390, projected 7% growth, bachelor's typical with under five years of related experience (S52).
- **Salary:** about $38K start, $52K to $93K mid-to-late career (S38), $80,390 BLS manager median (S52). Executive-director pay was not sourced.

## Uncertainties and Regional Variation

- **US focus.** Degree structure, funding, and salaries here are US. No non-US source was retrieved.
- **Vendor sources.** Extern (S38) is a career-services vendor and supplies the law-feeder framing, the $55K to $80K data-analyst range, several salary ranges, and the unverified underemployment claim. Pepperdine (S14) is a competitor's program page. Treat both as indicative.
- **Aggregator quality.** Glassdoor, PayScale, and Salary.com figures are self-reported or modeled. They sit below the BLS median for "political scientists" because the populations differ.
- **BLS data year.** Search excerpts on 2026-11-22 showed May 2024 figures. Direct fetches later the same day showed May 2025 figures with 2025-35 projections. The roadmap uses May 2025. The next BLS release will move these again.
- **Dated items.** LSAC applicants by major is 2015-16 (S48). The 34% top-five figure is from 2004 (S27). Claremont Lincoln's pay figures date to 2024 (S39).
- **Approximate figures.** APSA placement shares come from a fetch summary of a page that gave no exact percentages (S28).
- **Snippet-only evidence.** Most university requirement pages, program pages, and several salary pages were read as search excerpts, not full fetches (see "Seen as").
- **Unverified claims removed.** The 51% first-year underemployment figure could not be confirmed. The NY Fed page's outcomes-by-major table does not render in text (S56), and the St. Louis Fed article using NY Fed 2023 data lists criminal justice, performing arts, medical technicians, liberal arts, and anthropology as the five highest underemployment majors and gives no political science figure (S55).
- **Missing figures.** State Department FSO salary by grade (S54 gives rules only), lobbyist pay, master's tuition, communications-director pay, executive-director pay, and any recent political-science-specific tenure-track rate for PhD graduates.
- **AI exposure.** Sourced for law and for political data only. The JSX labels the other six as unsourced estimates.
- **Location.** Policy jobs concentrate in Washington, D.C. (S40, secondary source).
